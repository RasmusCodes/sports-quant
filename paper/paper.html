Do models beat the closing line? Probability models against exchange and sportsbook prices in the NBA and NFL

Research note, November 2026. Every number below is generated from the result files in outputs/ by scripts/build_paper.py; the code and data pipeline are in this repository.

Abstract

We assemble a leakage-controlled research platform from open data: NBA and NFL results, box scores, official injury reports with publication timestamps, sportsbook closing lines (NFL), and order-book price histories from two prediction-market exchanges, Kalshi and Polymarket. We fit Elo, Glicko-2, Poisson, a Bayesian dynamic state-space model, player-availability adjustments, walk-forward logistic regression and gradient boosting, and compare them with margin-free market probabilities. Across 14,338 out-of-sample NBA games the best model, an ensemble, lowers log loss relative to FiveThirtyEight-style Elo by 0.0161 (95% CI 0.0132 to 0.0190). Against Kalshi closing prices on 1,556 games the same ensemble is worse by 0.0158 (95% CI 0.0082 to 0.0228), and in a forecast-encompassing regression its weight is -0.10 (SE 0.17): the closing price already contains everything the model knows. The NFL tells the same story against devigged sportsbook closing moneylines (3,685 games). Schedule, fatigue and late injury news move prices but leave no detectable residual in closing prices after correcting for 24 simultaneous tests. Day-ahead prices are less efficient: the model's T-24h disagreement predicts the price's move to the close, and bets placed 24 hours out beat the closing price by +1.06 points on average (95% CI +0.72 to +1.40), but that is less than Kalshi's taker fee and spread. Pre-registered fractional-Kelly strategies built on model-versus-market disagreement lose money after costs, and the one decision rule that respects the evidence, a walk-forward combination of model and price, mostly abstains. The one apparent exception, a player-points model that seemed to add information to Kalshi prop prices, was put to a pre-registered replication on 728 held-out games. It passed as registered (one-sided p = 0.0005), but a post-hoc check traced the signal in both samples to a scoring error: props on players who sat out were scored as losses, while Kalshi settles them at a pre-game fair price. Scored as they settle, the model's weight is 0.02 (SE 0.05). The result is a careful negative: on these markets, public-information models do not find mispricing at the close that survives costs.

1. Question and approach

Sports betting markets are a clean laboratory for market efficiency: every contract settles on an observable outcome within hours. The research question is whether probabilities built from public information systematically disagree with market prices in a way that the outcomes vindicate. We deliberately do not optimise for historical return; the yardstick is out-of-sample forecast accuracy measured with proper scoring rules, calibration, forecast encompassing, and closing-line value (CLV), with betting simulations used only as a stress test of those measurements under realistic costs.

Three design choices drive the results:

  1. Margin-free market probabilities. Bookmaker prices are devigged with five methods (multiplicative, additive, power, Shin, odds-ratio). Exchange prices are mids of executable order books; Kalshi's two team-books are combined, and their ask-implied overround is reported as the exchange analogue of vig.
  2. Point-in-time information. Every input carries a timestamp. Ratings update only with results from earlier calendar days; injury reports count only if published before the decision time; bets trade the ask that existed at the decision time.
  3. Out-of-sample by construction. Rating hyperparameters are fitted on 2004-2015 (NBA) and 2002-2012 (NFL); learned models are walk-forward by season; the betting rules were fixed before any backtest was run.

3. Models

  • Elo with FiveThirtyEight's published NBA/NFL settings (K = 20, home edge 100 / 48 Elo points, margin-of-victory multiplier with autocorrelation correction, 75% / 67% carry-over) as the benchmark, and a version tuned by log loss on the training seasons.
  • Glicko-2 with per-game rating periods and deviation growth over idle days.
  • Poisson attack/defence with exponential time decay, refitted every game day; win probabilities read out both as Skellam and as a Gaussian margin with an empirically tracked residual SD.
  • Bayesian dynamic state-space model: team offence and defence follow random walks, league scoring level and home advantage are latent states, and a hierarchical prior partially pools teams toward the league mean each summer. Exact Kalman filtering yields the posterior after each game and the marginal likelihood used to fit hyperparameters by empirical Bayes; the predictive distribution gives moneyline, spread and total probabilities jointly.
  • Player availability (NBA): pre-game Game Score per 36 minutes and expected minutes for every player give the value a team loses when a player sits; expected absences come from the last official injury report before the decision time and are measured against what the ratings already absorbed. Starting quarterback (NFL): pre-game EPA per play of the announced starter versus the previous starter.
  • Walk-forward logistic regression and LightGBM on rating outputs, schedule, travel, altitude and availability features, and a margin-adjusted state-space model; the ensemble averages the three in log-odds. Three information sets (injury reports up to T-24h, T-6h, T-5m) are kept separate.
  • Monte Carlo simulators: a possession model for the NBA (with player-points allocation for props) and a drive model for the NFL that reproduces scoring in 3s and 7s.

Fitted rating hyperparameters (training seasons only) are in outputs/<league>/params.json; for example the tuned NBA Elo uses K = 9.6 and a home edge of 82 Elo points, and the state-space model's fitted season carry-over is 0.61.

The market prices it: each unit of late injury news between T-24h and T-5m moves the Kalshi price 0.0268 log-odds against the team that lost the player (descriptive regression, p < 0.001), and none of it is left in the closing price.

Day-ahead prices drift toward public information. Using only information available 24 hours before tip-off, the gap between the ensemble and the Kalshi price predicts how the price moves before the close: the price closes 0.17 of the log-odds gap (SE 0.02, R-squared 0.07); on Polymarket, whose day-ahead book is thinner, 0.44 (SE 0.05). Prices drift toward public information during the last day and arrive at a close that contains it. Section 9 shows the drift is real but smaller than the cost of trading on it.

A scoring error behind both results (found after the replication; not pre-registered). The registered code scores every prop from the box score, so a player who does not play counts as a No. Kalshi does not settle these markets No: the contract terms say a player who is active but never takes the court settles at the last fair market price before the game, and in the data every prop on a player who did not play, including players listed Out, was settled at such a price (result "scalar"; mean settlement value 0.43 in the replication sample). Those props carry essentially the whole signal. The replication sample has 74 of them (16 in discovery), 52 on players the injury report had already listed Out, which the model, reading the report, prices at its 0.5% floor. Across the scalar-settled props the closing mid averages 0.39 (mean spread 20 points): the market expects a fair-price settlement, not a zero. Below, the same analyses are scored the way the markets settled: scalar-settled props are left out of the 0/1 regression and paid at their settlement value in the trading rules (2 further scalar-settled props, on players who did play, are treated the same way); the model, filters, frozen coefficients and costs are unchanged (sq/research/props_exploratory.py).

Scored as settled, the player model adds nothing to the closing price in either sample: its weight is 0.022 (SE 0.047) on the held-out games and 0.11 (SE 0.12) in discovery, the frozen combination scores the same as the market, and every trading rule loses money on the held-out games. The outcome was mismeasured, identically in both samples, because the replication reused the frozen discovery code. Pre-registration guards against selective analysis, not against an error shared by discovery and replication. Spread and total outcomes were checked against each market's own settlement (above); the props outcome should have been too, and the settlement-consistent numbers are the ones to rely on. The same audit of the game-winner markets finds nothing to correct: none of the 3 NBA games Kalshi settled at a scalar price matches a game played on its date, and the 2 NFL games that do match are ties (settled at 0.50), which are already excluded from scoring. The props pipeline (sq/research/nba_lines.py) now scores outcomes from settlement by default and reproduces the discovery "as settled" column exactly; the box-score outcome is kept as a check and agrees with 100% of binary settlements. The registered code is preserved byte-for-byte as sq/research/nba_lines_registered.py, with its outputs in outputs/nba/registered/.

9. Backtests

Pre-registered rule, fixed before any run: quarter Kelly on the model's probability, a minimum edge of 2 points after all costs, at most 2% of bankroll per bet and 15% per day, bets sized off the start-of-day bankroll, Kalshi taker fees (0.07 x P x (1 - P) per contract, rounded up per order), executable asks only, stake at most 2% of the game's pre-tip volume. Each decision time uses its own information set.

Reading the table:

  • A model that is less accurate than the market still disagrees with it on most games, and Kelly sizing treats every disagreement as edge. The ensemble's T-6h picks are bought at roughly the closing fair price: CLV, measured against the ask actually paid, is +0.15 points. The taker fee (1.41 points per contract on average) turns that into -1.26, so the expected return at closing prices is -3.1% and the realised -6.1% is that expectation plus noise.
  • The risk limits did not prevent deep drawdowns, because the binding risk is a biased probability, not bet size. A strategy's edge estimate is only as good as its probability, and here the probability is worse than the price it bets against.
  • The day-ahead drift of Section 6 is visible in the T-24h strategy: its bets beat the close by +1.06 points on average (95% CI +0.72 to +1.40), the only reliably positive CLV in the study. Kalshi's taker fee (1.44 points) is larger, so CLV after costs is -0.38 points and the expected return at closing prices -1.3%: predictable drift that stays inside the band trading costs allow, which is what efficiency net of costs predicts. Whether resting limit orders (lower maker fee, no spread crossing) could keep the drift is open; testing it needs order-book depth and fill data that are not in this dataset.
  • The combination rule estimates, from earlier games only, how much weight the model deserves next to the price. Because that weight is close to zero, the combined probability collapses onto the price and the rule places 122 bets out of 1,265 eligible games.

A leakage check. A first run fed the T-24h strategy with injury information from T-6h, up to 18 hours of news the market had not yet seen. That version showed a CLV of +1.21 points against +1.06 with the correct T-24h information set, and a return of -2.1% against -1.5%. The look-ahead inflated CLV, though here most of the T-24h CLV turned out to be the genuine drift above. The correct pairing of decision time and information set is enforced in code and tested.

Sensitivity (outputs/nba/backtest_grid.csv): Kelly fractions 0.1-0.5, minimum edges 1-5 points and slippage of 0 or 1 point are reported for every strategy; they were not used to choose the configuration above.

10. Limitations

  • Short exchange history. NBA exchange prices span parts of three seasons; the 95% intervals above are the honest measure of what that sample can say. A sportsbook history for the NBA would lengthen it, but the free one available forbids use without consent.
  • Venue costs. Polymarket's fee schedule for sports has changed over time; its backtests assume no fee and a one-point half-spread, and report sensitivity instead of a single number.
  • Timing approximations. NBA tip-off times come from the ESPN schedule redistributed by sportsdataverse; NFL starting quarterbacks are the actual starters (normally announced before kickoff); NFL wind is game-time weather. Each is flagged where it enters.
  • Player-availability proxy. Before the 2018-19 season (no injury-report archive) availability is read from the game's own inactive list, which is public before tip-off but would not be available at T-24h; those seasons are training data only.
  • Props. The discovery sample is a random 15% of games (125 after matching) and the replication uses the rest (728); both cover January to November 2026, so the replication is out of sample in games but not in time. The registered props code scored outcomes from the box score, while Kalshi settles props on players who do not play at a pre-game fair price; Section 8's settlement-consistent numbers are the ones to rely on, and the default props pipeline now scores that way.

11. Reproducibility

README.md lists the commands that rebuild every table and figure from an empty cache; raw responses are cached with their fetch time and hash in data/raw/*/_manifest.jsonl. The leakage tests in tests/test_leakage.py perturb future results, same-day results, test-season outcomes, late injury reports and closing prices and verify that no earlier prediction or bet changes.
